\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Virtual avatars stand in for people across games, telepresence and other digital services. Their visual design shapes how they are perceived. That perception gains importance in the context of the uncanny valley, since an avatar that approaches human likeness without reaching it can trigger discomfort instead of familiarity. Non-photorealistic rendering~(NPR) offers a principled alternative, as it abstracts appearance deliberately and positions an avatar along the fidelity axis. That placement carries consequences for perceived trust and whether such abstraction preserves it remains unresolved.

This thesis presents the design, implementation and evaluation of real-time NPR stylisation for virtual avatars. Eight techniques were developed iteratively, each motivated by a limitation of the one before it: a geometric silhouette outline, toon shading and its X-Toon extension, screen-space normal edge detection, Sobel edge detection, Gaussian prefiltered Sobel, hierarchical edge detection over depth, normal and colour discontinuities, and anisotropic Kuwahara painterly filtering. The techniques were implemented and compared across three avatar platforms: a Mixamo humanoid model, the Meta Avatars SDK and Avaturn. This revealed their visual behaviour and stability under different mesh topologies and rendering pipelines.

A parameter adjustment study with 45 participants calibrated the candidate techniques. They ranked configurations round by round, and the outcome retained the geometric silhouette outline, X-Toon tonal shading and Sobel edge detection. In the main study of trust and the uncanny valley effect, 41 participants watched short video scenarios in which a speaker recommended one of two options in a practical decision. The speaker appeared either as a real person, a photorealistic Avaturn avatar or the NPR composite. Each was rated on eeriness, likeability and three dimensions of trust: ability, benevolence and integrity. Participants registered the two avatar conditions as differently stylised. However, the two avatars did not differ significantly on any scale. By contrast, the real speaker was rated less eerie, more likeable and more trustworthy than either.

An equivalence test on the trust composite was inconclusive at this sample size. Therefore, the two avatars are indistinguishable rather than demonstrably equivalent. Nonetheless, eeriness predicted lower trust within participants on all three dimensions, and likeability predicted higher trust by a comparable amount. Both are proposed measures of the vertical axis of the uncanny valley, and this supports the view that an avatar's position on that axis also predicts trust. These findings qualify the expectation that NPR abstraction alone relocates an avatar out of that valley.
